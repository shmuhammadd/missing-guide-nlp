# SomaliClinicalNLP — Technical Response

**Workstream 2: Model Development** · Dr Shamsuddeen Hassan Muhammad · 3 June 2026

Assembling the pipeline — STT, an LLM, TTS, a dialogue loop — is the easy part. The hard problems are that Somali is nearly absent from both speech and clinical NLP, and that the clinical signal lives in culturally encoded idioms no ontology contains. Every choice below is built to extract maximum value from a small, clinician-validated corpus: the dataset is the asset, not the model. I draw throughout on my own work in low-resource African ASR and African-language conversational AI.

## 1. Base models

**STT.** Somali is not in Whisper's published language list, and on unseen languages off-the-shelf Whisper can exceed 100% WER (Pashto; Rahman, 2026), so fine-tuning is a baseline requirement, not an option. I would benchmark two strong multilingual priors zero-shot, then adapt the better one: Meta **MMS** (`mms-1b-all`, which already ships a Somali adapter and more than halves Whisper's WER across 54 FLEURS languages; Pratap et al., 2023) and **Whisper-large-v2**. I would adapt with parameter-efficient fine-tuning rather than full fine-tuning: in our AfricaNLP 2026 study, **DoRA matched full fine-tuning on Hausa, Yoruba and Igbo (22.0 vs 22.1 WER) while training ~60× fewer parameters** on ~10h each (Imam et al., 2026) — the right economics for scarce audio and limited GPUs.

**LLM.** A generic Llama 3 will not speak clinical Somali safely. I would QLoRA-fine-tune Llama 3.1 8B Instruct (for clinical information extraction, LoRA reached entity-F1 0.78 and 4-bit QLoRA came within 2–4 points at ~24 GB GPU RAM; clinical QLoRA study, 2025) and RAG-ground it in the validated expression dictionary. Domain adaptation lets small open models rival much larger ones — continued medical pretraining lifted Meditron's MedQA from 63.8 to 70.2 (Chen et al., 2023) — though its authors rightly warn against clinical deployment without trials, a caution I carry into this work.

**TTS.** VITS is the right default: single-stage, MOS 4.43 on 24h of single-speaker English, and it degrades gracefully with little data (Kim et al., 2021). A Somali VITS checkpoint already exists (`facebook/mms-tts-som`), but under a non-commercial licence, so I would prototype from it and build licence-clean data for release (see Q4).

## 2. Fine-tuning ASR with limited data

Never train from scratch — the central conclusion of our systematic review of African ASR, where **18 of the surveyed datasets hold under 10 hours** (Imam et al., 2025a). My recipe: (1) adapt MMS/Whisper with DoRA/LoRA, not full fine-tuning; (2) pseudo-label untranscribed Somali audio, exactly how Biswas et al. (2019) grew a **1.57h Somali seed** to usable training data with 17.55h of untranscribed speech; (3) augment for phone and voice-note conditions (SpecAugment, speed/pitch perturbation, additive noise) — a robustness point we stress for African deployment (Imam et al., 2025b); (4) fuse a Somali text language model, which is decisive in the low-data regime (Baevski et al., 2020); and (5) normalize Somali orthography before training and scoring — corpus mixing and homophone normalization were the two biggest levers in our Amharic Whisper work (Gete et al., 2025). I would report **CER and a diacritic-aware metric alongside WER**, since WER alone misleads for morphologically rich languages. Realistic expectation: ~50h of clean transcribed speech reaches practical WER for a comparable African language (Kinyarwanda; Sunbird AI, 2025); no published Somali benchmark exists, so I would establish one. For confidentiality, federated/on-device training is worth piloting for sensitive clinical audio.

## 3. Mapping cultural narratives to structured outputs

This is the scientific core, and why Workstream 1 outranks any model choice. "Fire running from my stomach" is not noise to translate away — it is a culturally encoded idiom of distress that maps to a clinical concept only through cultural knowledge. I would build two layers. **(a) Interpretation:** tag each narrative span with the DSM-5 cultural framework — *cultural syndrome*, *idiom of distress*, or *causal attribution* — because these deliberately do not map one-to-one onto SNOMED/ICD; the Somali expression dataset becomes a bilingual gazetteer (idiom → candidate symptom) that feeds this layer. **(b) Normalization:** link interpreted mentions to UMLS/SNOMED CUIs with an embedding-retrieval model such as **SapBERT (96.5% top-1 on MedMentions with no task fine-tuning; Liu et al., 2021)** — ideal when labelled Somali→CUI pairs are scarce. The output schema is explicit: symptom, body system, severity, temporality, and **negation as its own field** (negation must be modelled, not inferred). Two empirical cautions: a domain encoder fine-tuned on ~150 annotated narratives beat zero-shot LLMs at symptom extraction (F1 0.84 vs 0.45), and even tuned BERT models catch only 25–50% of figurative phrasings — so the curated dictionary, not the model, closes the cultural gap. I would therefore use a QLoRA-tuned Llama 3 for the generative mapping but keep it RAG-grounded and schema-constrained — the hallucination-control pattern from our African travel assistant (Ngoufo, Muhammad & Fokoa, 2026).

## 4. TTS for natural spoken questions

Fine-tune a pretrained VITS rather than train from scratch: with transfer learning, ~30 minutes of target-speaker audio can match a from-scratch model trained on 27 hours, and ~20h of clean single-speaker data already gives MOS ~4.0 in a low-resource language. For licence-clean Somali data I would reuse the **BibleTTS recipe I contributed to (Meyer et al., 2022)**: force-align existing single-speaker Somali recordings (e.g. scripture readings) into studio-quality 48 kHz, openly licensed TTS data. Because the assistant asks clinical follow-ups, I would pre-synthesize a clinician-approved bank of question templates and human-rate them rather than rely on open-ended generation, locking pronunciation of numbers, units and anatomy with G2P rules. Evaluate with subjective MOS plus an objective proxy — run the synthesized speech back through the Somali ASR and check WER for intelligibility.

## 5. Iterative diagnostic dialogue

I would not let the LLM free-associate toward a diagnosis. The LLM conducts the Somali conversation, but the next question is chosen against an explicit symptom–disease belief state to **maximize information gain** — the interpretable, data-light logic of classic symptom checkers and of Wei et al.'s (2018) reinforcement-learning symptom-acquisition agent, where follow-ups beyond the initial complaint sharply raised accuracy. AMIE shows how strong LLM history-taking can be (the correct diagnosis was in its differential 90% of the time; Tu et al., 2025) but also the failure modes I must engineer against — overconfidence and premature action, seen in dozens of patients in clinical testing. So I would build hard guardrails: keep questioning while diagnostic uncertainty exceeds threshold, defer explicitly to a clinician, and **never emit a diagnosis** — the system's output is a structured symptom report for a doctor, matching the Workstream 2 report-generation step. With no Somali medical-dialogue corpus in existence (the large ones are Chinese/English), I would bootstrap training dialogues by self-play over the validated expression dataset, then refine with clinician review.

## 6. Evaluation without a large labelled set

Build a small, expert-adjudicated gold set, not a large noisy one. I would mirror a recent clinical-ASR protocol that worked on only ~300 clinician-labelled pairs (*WER is Unaware*, 2026): two clinicians label each item, report inter-annotator agreement, and adjudicate disagreements into a reconciled gold standard. For agreement on skewed clinical labels I would use **Gwet's AC1 or Krippendorff's α rather than Cohen's κ**, which collapses under class imbalance (the kappa paradox). I would evaluate at three levels: ASR with WER/CER plus a clinician-rated clinical-distortion scale (WER weights all errors equally and misses meaning-inverting ones); structured mapping against physician-assigned labels (per-field precision/recall, top-k differential accuracy); and end-to-end OSCE-style dialogues scored by Somali clinicians. An LLM-as-judge can scale this — a tuned judge reached κ = 0.816 against clinicians in the ASR study — but I would treat it as an assistant, not an oracle: judge scores swing across runs (reported IRR 0.167–1.0) and carry model-specific bias, so I would use multiple seeds, anchor every judge to a human-labelled subset, and report dialect/region breakdowns so the system is never validated on a single variety of Somali.

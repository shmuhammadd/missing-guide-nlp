The rest of this guide assumes you are already in a lab with a result and a deadline. This chapter is for the step before that: getting into a graduate program in NLP. Read it if you are an undergraduate, a master's student, a research assistant, or an engineer thinking about a PhD. Advisors can hand it to the students who email them every autumn.

The chapter is opinionated about what matters and short on things you can look up. Where other people have written the definitive piece on a topic, we link to it. The [further reading](#further-reading) section is a curated list, and two community-maintained collections go much deeper: Shaily Bhatt's [advice](https://github.com/shaily99/advice) repository and Zhijing Jin's [Resources to Help Global Equality for PhDs in NLP](https://github.com/zhijing-jin/nlp-phd-global-equality).

## Decide what you are applying for

A **PhD** is a research job with training attached. You produce papers, talks and code for four to six years, and you are paid a stipend to do it. John Hewitt puts it this way in [Undergrad to PhD, or not](https://www.cs.columbia.edu/~johnhew/undergrad-researchers.html): a PhD is a job whose deliverables are units of research. If you do not want that job, a PhD is an expensive way to find out.

A **research master's** gives you a year or two of coursework and a thesis. In many countries it is the normal entry route to a PhD. In the US it is usually self-funded and is not required for PhD admission.

A **pre-doctoral or research assistant position** pays you to do research for one or two years before applying. Google Research, the Allen Institute for AI, Microsoft Research and many university labs run them. They exist to turn promising people into strong applicants. Shaily Bhatt's [account of the pre-doctoral role at Google Research India](https://shaily99.medium.com/demystifying-the-pre-doctoral-researcher-role-at-google-research-india-c063e5c73f10) shows what the work looks like from inside.

Large language models changed what NLP research looks like, and many applicants now ask whether a PhD is still worth it. Read [A PhD Student's Perspective on Research in NLP in the Era of Very Large Language Models](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.12544) (Ignat et al., 2023). Its authors are PhD students, and the paper lists open problems that do not need a frontier-scale compute budget. Emily Bender's [Is it worth it to go to grad school in computational linguistics?](https://medium.com/@emilymenonbender/is-it-worth-it-to-go-to-grad-school-in-computational-linguistics-7234f0bd4981) covers the linguistics side of the field.

## What admissions committees look for

A PhD admissions committee is trying to predict one thing: will this person do good research? Every part of your application is read as evidence for or against that prediction.

The strongest evidence comes from people who have watched you do research. Nelson Liu and colleagues surveyed twelve recently admitted NLP PhD students for [Student Perspectives on Applying to NLP PhD Programs](https://blog.nelsonliu.me/2019/10/24/student-perspectives-on-applying-to-nlp-phd-programs/). Every respondent ranked letters of recommendation as the most or second-most important part of the application. Mor Harchol-Balter's [Applying to Ph.D. Programs in Computer Science](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~harchol/gradschooltalk.pdf) gives the same answer from a CMU faculty member's chair.

Publications help, but they stand in for something else. Liu wrote in the same survey that papers signal whether you "can complete the full research process, from idea inception to experiment execution to writing things up." A strong letter describing a project that has not yet been published can carry the same weight. A workshop paper, a shared-task system description, a careful code release or a thoughtful blog post all count as evidence.

Grades and test scores act as thresholds. Most survey respondents thought they mattered little once they cleared a minimum. Many US programs no longer require the GRE at all. Check each program's page, because the rules change from year to year.

## Build a research record before you apply

Start research as early as you can. In Liu's survey, many respondents met at least one letter writer only in their third or fourth undergraduate year, and several said they wished they had started sooner. A letter writer needs months of shared work to write a specific letter.

You do not need a famous lab to start. These routes work, and the rest of this guide supports each of them:

- **A project with a faculty member at your own institution.** Ask for a small, well-defined task. Doing it well is how you earn a bigger one.
- **A shared task.** The data, the metric and the deadline are fixed, so you can focus on the system and the write-up. [Chapter 1](./01-publication-landscape.md#shared-tasks) explains how they work, and [Chapter 3](./03-paper-types.md) covers the system-description paper.
- **The Student Research Workshop.** ACL, NAACL, EMNLP and other conferences run an SRW for student work, often with pre-submission mentoring. See [Chapter 3](./03-paper-types.md).
- **A research community.** Groups such as [Masakhane](https://www.masakhane.io/) run open, distributed projects that welcome newcomers and produce \*ACL papers with dozens of authors. Contribute something real, such as annotation, a baseline or an analysis, and someone senior will see your work.
- **Summer research programs and internships.** Zhijing Jin's repository keeps a [list of pre-PhD research opportunities](https://github.com/zhijing-jin/nlp-phd-global-equality#prereq-getting-pre-phd-research-opportunities).

When you do write up the work, the chapters on [framing](./06-framing.md), [writing clearly](./08-writing-clearly.md) and [working with advisors](./07-collaboration.md) apply to your first paper as much as to anyone's. [Chapter 4](./04-contribution.md) explains what counts as a contribution. A first paper with a clear claim and an honest [limitations section](./22-limitations.md) is better evidence than a crowded one.

## Choose where to apply

Choose advisors, not universities. The respondents in Liu's survey named faculty fit as the strongest factor, and several applied only where at least two faculty members matched their interests. Two matches protect you if one professor is not taking students that year, or leaves.

Build the list from papers. [Chapter 2](./02-understanding-the-field.md) shows how to map a field by its people and venues. Open the last two years of ACL, EMNLP and NAACL proceedings on the [ACL Anthology](https://aclanthology.org/) and note whose work you keep reading. [CSRankings](https://csrankings.org/) lets you filter by the NLP area to see which faculty publish there. Treat its rankings as a search tool, not a verdict. Tim Dettmers's [How to Pick Your Grad School](https://timdettmers.com/2020/03/10/how-to-pick-your-grad-school/) gives a fuller method.

Systems differ by country. US programs admit you to a department, start with two years of coursework, and run five to six years. Most of the UK and continental Europe hire you into a specific advisor's funded position, expect a master's degree, and run three to four years. The [ELLIS PhD Program](https://ellis.eu/phd-postdoc) runs one central application for machine learning and NLP labs across Europe, with co-supervision in two countries. Its call usually opens in October.

Apply to more places than feels comfortable. Admissions in NLP are competitive and noisy. Include programs where you are a strong candidate as well as reach programs, but apply only to places you would attend.

## Contact faculty only when it helps

Most students in Liu's survey did not email faculty before applying. A generic email does not help and can hurt. Many professors say on their homepage whether they want to hear from applicants. Follow that instruction.

If you do write, make the email short and specific. Name a paper of theirs you have read, say in one sentence what you would want to work on, and attach a CV. Yonatan Bisk's [note on emailing professors](https://yonatanbisk.com/emailing_professors.html) explains when an email is worth sending. David Evans's [advice for prospective students](https://www.cs.virginia.edu/~evans/advice/prospective.html) shows what a professor sees in their inbox.

A conversation at a conference or workshop does more than any cold email. Bring a question about their recent work.

## The statement of purpose

The statement of purpose is a research document, not a personal essay. Its job is to show that you have done research, understood what you did, and know what you want to do next. In [Inside Ph.D. admissions: What readers look for in a Statement of Purpose](https://nschneid.medium.com/inside-ph-d-admissions-what-readers-look-for-in-a-statement-of-purpose-3db4e6081f80), Nathan Schneider describes how faculty actually read one.

Lead with your research. For each project, say what question you asked, what you did, what you found, and what you learned that changed how you work. Specific detail is what makes a reader trust you: the dataset, the language, the baseline that beat your first idea. "I have always been passionate about language" tells the reader nothing. Andy Pavlo's [How to Write a Bad Statement](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~pavlo/blog/2015/10/how-to-write-a-bad-statement-for-a-computer-science-phd-admissions-application.html) catalogs the openings that fail.

Then say what you want to work on. Name two or three directions and connect them to what you have done. Committees know that interests change. They want to see that you can pose a research question at all.

Tailor the last paragraph, not the whole statement. Most of Liu's respondents kept 80 to 90 percent of the text identical across schools. They changed the final paragraph to name two to four faculty members, with one sentence each on why.

Framing a statement uses the same skills as framing a paper. The questions in [Chapter 6](./06-framing.md) work here, and so does the sentence-level advice in [Chapter 8](./08-writing-clearly.md). Have a full draft at least a month before the first deadline. Send it to your letter writers so their letters and your statement tell the same story. Real examples help: [CS-SOP](https://cs-sop.notion.site/) collects statements from admitted CS PhD students, and Nelson Liu has published [his own statement with commentary](https://blog.nelsonliu.me/2020/11/11/phd-personal-statement/).

## Letters of recommendation

You need three letters. Choose people who supervised your research over people with famous names who only taught you. A letter that says "I taught this student in a class of 200, and they earned an A" adds almost nothing.

Make the letter easy to write. A month or more before the deadline, send each writer one email with your CV, your statement draft, the list of programs and deadlines, and a short reminder of what you did together. Matt Might's [How to get a great letter of recommendation](https://matt.might.net/articles/how-to-recommendation-letter/) and Michael Ernst's [Requesting a letter of recommendation](https://homes.cs.washington.edu/~mernst/advice/request-recommendation.html) describe what to include.

Then send reminders. Liu sent them two weeks, one week and a few days before each deadline. Your writers are busy and will thank you.

## CV and application logistics

Keep the CV to research, skills and anything that shows initiative. Put publications first if you have them, in a consistent citation format. Label each one honestly as published, accepted or under review, and never upgrade the status. Link to code and data you released.

Budget money and time. Application fees in the US often reach USD 100 per program, and test scores add more. Many programs waive fees on request. The [CS PhD application fee waivers](https://github.com/KaiserWhoLearns/CS-PhD-Application-fee-waivers) repository lists them, and [Queer in AI](https://www.queerinai.com/grad-app-aid) runs a fee-support program. A funded PhD in computer science should cost you nothing in tuition. If a program asks you to pay for a PhD, ask why.

## Get feedback from a review program

Many universities run free, student-led programs that review statements and CVs before you submit. Each takes applications in the early autumn and closes before the December deadlines. Examples include CMU's [Graduate Application Support Program](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/academics/phd/application-support), Columbia's [PAR program](https://www.cs.columbia.edu/cscu-phd-par-program/) and the University of Washington's [PAMS](https://www.cs.washington.edu/academics/phd/admissions/pams). Most give priority to applicants from groups underrepresented in computing. Andrew Kuznetsov maintains a [tracker of these programs](https://www.andrewkuz.net/public/dashboards/cs-hci-phd-application-review-programs/2024/html/cs-hci-phd-application-review-programs-2024.html).

Mentoring programs run longer. The [ACL Year-Round Mentorship](https://mentorship.aclweb.org/) program holds monthly sessions with NLP researchers. [Black in AI](https://www.blackinai.org/our-work) runs a graduate-preparation program for Black applicants with mentoring and application review.

## If you are applying from an under-resourced region

Most advice on the web assumes an applicant at a well-known US or European university. If you are applying from Africa, South Asia, Latin America or elsewhere, the process is the same, but some parts are harder, and some resources exist for you specifically.

Your letter writers may not be known to the committee. Make their letters specific: concrete projects and concrete comparisons ("the strongest of the 40 students I have supervised") carry weight that a famous name would otherwise supply. A collaborator at a better-known institution, met through a shared task or a community project, can add a letter that the committee recognizes.

Compute is not the barrier it seems. Many good first projects in NLP need data and care, not GPUs. Datasets, evaluation, analysis and work on under-served languages are all strong first papers, and you may be one of the few people placed to do them well.

A funded master's can bridge the gap. The [African Master's in Machine Intelligence](https://aimsammi.org/) at AIMS is a fully funded one-year program for African students. The [Deep Learning Indaba](https://deeplearningindaba.com/) and regional summer schools such as [EEML](https://www.eeml.eu/) put you in the room with researchers who write letters and hire students. Zhijing Jin's [repository](https://github.com/zhijing-jin/nlp-phd-global-equality) was written for exactly this situation.

## Interviews and decisions

Some programs interview shortlisted applicants, often between January and March. Expect a professor to ask about a project on your CV in detail. Be ready to explain why you made each choice and what you would do differently. Prepare questions of your own.

When offers arrive, talk to current students in each lab, without the professor present. Ask how often they meet their advisor, how long students take to graduate, and where recent graduates went. Andrew Kuznetsov's [Questions to Ask a Prospective Ph.D. Advisor on Visit Day](https://blog.ml.cmu.edu/2020/03/02/questions-to-ask-a-prospective-ph-d-advisor-on-visit-day-with-thorough-and-forthright-explanations/) is the best list we know. Compare stipends against local living costs; [CS PhD Stipends](https://csstipendrankings.org/) does the arithmetic for many US programs. The advisor matters more than the ranking. [Chapter 7](./07-collaboration.md) describes the relationship you are choosing.

## A timeline

US deadlines fall mostly between 1 and 15 December for a start the following autumn. European positions open throughout the year. Work backwards from your earliest deadline.

| When | What to do |
|---|---|
| 18 to 12 months before | Start or deepen a research project. Aim for work a letter writer can describe in detail. |
| 12 to 6 months before | Push the project toward a submission, a workshop paper or a public release. Start reading recent papers from labs you like. |
| 6 to 4 months before | Draft the list of programs and faculty. Book any required tests. |
| 3 months before | Ask your letter writers. Apply to application-review programs. Write the first full draft of your statement. |
| 1 month before | Final statement draft to letter writers. Request fee waivers. Finish the CV. |
| Deadline weeks | Submit early. Send reminders to letter writers. |
| January to April | Interviews, offers, visits. Most US programs ask you to decide by 15 April. |

## Common mistakes

- **Choosing letter writers by fame.** A detailed letter from a lecturer who supervised your thesis beats a generic letter from a dean.
- **A statement that tells your life story.** Committees read for evidence of research. Start with the research.
- **Naming a professor you have not read.** If you list someone in your final paragraph, cite something they have written in the last two years.
- **Mass cold emails.** A templated email sent to 50 professors gets deleted 50 times.
- **Waiting for the perfect profile.** Each year the bar moves. If you can show research ability now, apply now.
- **Applying only to the top ten.** Noise in admissions is high. Fit at a lab outside the top ten beats a rejection from inside it.
